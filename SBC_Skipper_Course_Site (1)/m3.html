Module 3 - Rules of the water

SBC Skipper · Module 3 of 8 · ≈ 75 minutes · Quiz: 12 questions from a 46-question bank, 80% to pass

These are the common small-boat licence topics, adapted to New Zealand. There's nobody out there checking. These rules only work because skippers like you know them and use them.

Lesson 3.1 — Look out & slow down ≈ 7 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M3-01a looking out from the helm

Two habits cover most of boating safety:

Keep looking. You're the eyes of the boat. Other boats, swimmers, kayakers, divers, floating logs — it's your job to see them early. At speed, watch ahead and let a crew member watch the sides. Never assume the other skipper has seen you. Most probably haven't.

Go slow when it makes sense. The speed limits below are the maximum, not the target. Fog, glare, chop, busy water, or a nervous crew — all good reasons to go slower. Simple test: if you couldn't stop in the water you can see ahead of you, you're going too fast.

Your wash is your problem too. If the wave behind your boat tips someone's dinghy or soaks a fisherman — that's on you. Slow right down near moored boats, kayakers and anyone close to the water.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou want to check the map screen while driving at speed. What do you do first? (Slow down or stop — screens and speed don't mix. Same rule as your phone in the car.)

Lesson 3.2 — The 5-knot rule ≈ 7 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTGRAPHIC - M3-02 5-knot zone diagram

5 knots is about walking pace. Stay at 5 knots or slower:

  • »»within 50 metres of any other boat or person in the water
  • »»within 200 metres of the shore or anything built — wharves, ramps, moorings, marine farms
  • »»within 200 metres of a boat flying the divers flag
  • »»whenever anyone on board is hanging over the side
  • »»anywhere else a sign or buoy says so — some spots have extra slow zones

It counts when you're towing someone too. Marinas are all wharves and moorings — so think "walking pace" for the whole marina. Getting this wrong costs $200, and the harbour patrol does check.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA boat is anchored with its dive flag up, 150 m off the beach. What does that mean for you? (A 200 m slow-zone around that boat — go around it or crawl past at 5 kn.)

Lesson 3.3 — Who goes first: power meets power ≈ 9 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M3-01b crossing diagram from the helm

When two engine boats come together, one gives way (lets the other go first — slow down and pass behind) and the other keeps going. Three situations:

Coming in from the side: if the other boat is on your right, you give way. Slow down and pass behind them.

Head-on: both boats turn to the right and pass left-side to left-side.

Passing them: coming up behind someone — even a sailboat — you're passing, and they keep their course. You keep clear.

Two habits that prevent most crashes:

  • »»Turn early and turn big. A small nudge of the wheel at distance looks like "maybe they'll move". A big, obvious turn says "I've seen you".
  • »»If they don't move — you still must. Having "right of way" means nothing if the other skipper isn't looking. The rule is: avoid the crash, whatever it takes.

The whole table, one place:

SituationWho gives way
Another power boat on your rightYou
You're on another boat's rightThey do
Head-on with a power boatBoth turn right
You're passing anyone (can see their back)You
A sail, paddle or oar boat crossingYou (unless you're passing them)
A yacht under motorTreat it as a power boat
A boat that can't manoeuvre (restricted, fishing with gear down)You — big clearance
In a riverUpstream boats let downstream boats come through

Two worked crossings:

  • »»Scenario A: You're heading to Motuihe; a launch is crossing from your right, 300 m off, steady bearing. You're the give-way boat — slow now, turn right early and pass well behind her stern. A steady bearing + closing distance = you're colliding unless one of you acts, and that one is you.
  • »»Scenario B: Same crossing, but the launch is on your LEFT. She gives way to you — but you hold a steady, predictable course and speed so her skipper can see exactly what you're doing. If she doesn't act, you act: turn right (away) or slow hard.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA boat is approaching from your right at a crossing angle. Who slows first? (You do — the boat on your right goes first. Pass behind them, early and clearly.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou're coming up behind a slow fishing boat and can see its back. Who keeps clear? (You're passing them — you keep clear, every time.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA yacht under sail is crossing ahead, but it's flying a black cone pointed DOWN. What does that tell you? (It's motorsailing/fishing or showing a working shape — its status may differ from what it looks like. Read the shapes, give it extra room, and don't rely on "engines give way to sails".)

Lesson 3.4 — Sails, paddles & rivers ≈ 6 min

  • »»Engines give way to sails, paddles and oars — unless you're passing them. A yacht under sail goes first. (A yacht with its engine on behaves like an engine boat — whatever its sails say.)
  • »»But watch what they're showing. Some boats fly shapes or lights that change the normal order — a yacht motoring shows a black cone, and some big or broken-down ships can't move at all. If a boat looks restricted or is showing odd signals, stay well clear. Don't rely on the normal order at their expense.
  • »»Sail vs sail: the boat with the wind coming over its left side gives way. Same side as the other boat? The one further upwind gives way.
  • »»Rivers: keep to the right side of the channel, and boats going upstream let the downstream boats come through — they've got more control coming down with the flow.

You'll share the water with yachts, kayaks, waka and paddleboarders all day. Give them room early — they can't hear you over your engine.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA yacht under full sail crosses ahead of you. Who slows? (You — engines give way to sails, unless you're passing them.)

Lesson 3.5 — Lights: reading other boats at dusk ≈ 7 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M3-01d boat lights at dusk

SBC boats are moored before dark — but dusk runs are common, and the half-hour around sunset is when you need to read other boats by their lights:

  • »»Red light on their left side, green on their right (sidelights). If you see red and green together, you're looking at their front — head-on: both of you turn right.
  • »»You see a red light crossing ahead of you — you're looking at their port side, which puts them on your starboard side: you give way (same as the day rule).
  • »»A single white light as you approach from behind = you're overtaking: you keep clear — and don't assume it's a stern light: a small powerboat may be showing an all-round white that hides its sidelights from directly behind. Keep clear and pass wide.
  • »»All-round white light with NO sidelights, not moving = likely at anchor — slow down and give room; there may be people asleep aboard.
  • »»All-round white light WITH sidelights = a small power vessel under way (boats under 12 m may show this instead of masthead and stern lights) — treat it like any power boat: give way as the day rules say.
  • »»White light high up, red and green lower = a bigger power vessel — give way early if crossing.
  • »»The rule of thumb: never decide "anchored" from a white light alone — check for sidelights and movement, and slow down either way.

And the flip side: your own lights — red and green sidelights, white stern light — tell other skippers exactly what you are. Check them at pre-departure even for a dusk run.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou're crossing and see another boat's red sidelight on your right. Who gives way? (You — their port side faces you, putting them on your starboard side. Slow and pass behind.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA single all-round white light ahead — no sidelights, not moving. Likely at anchor: slow down and give room; people may be asleep aboard. Careful: a small powerboat under way may also show an all-round white WITH sidelights — never decide from a white light alone.

Lesson 3.6 — Big ships ≈ 7 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M3-01c large ship passing

A loaded ship can't see a small boat well from up on the bridge, turns slowly, and takes a very long time to stop — and with cargo aboard, it often has no room to move out of the deep channel. Boats under 15 metres, yachts, kayaks — the little boat always moves first. That's the rule and it's good sense.

  • »»Stay 500 metres ahead of a big ship on the move.
  • »»Stay 200 metres off the sides and back of tankers.
  • »»Around the biggest ships in Auckland there's an exclusion box: 100 metres to the sides, 100 metres behind, 500 metres ahead. Big fine, zero sympathy.
  • »»Keep right in the channel. Never anchor in it — or anywhere you'd block other boats or a wharf.
  • »»Never tie to, hit or move a channel marker. They keep boats alive.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA container ship is coming up the channel at 6 knots. You're doing 20 in a 6-metre boat. Who moves, and why? (You — they take kilometres to stop, can barely see you, and the rule puts the move on the small boat: 500 m ahead, out of the channel.)

Lesson 3.7 — Buoys & marks: the water's signposts ≈ 12 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTSTILLS - M3-04 buoy marks annotated
»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTPHOTO - buoy

NZ uses the red-left, green-right system (same as Europe). Five mark types cover everything:

1. Channel edge marks. Coming in from the sea: red cans on your LEFT, green cones on your RIGHT. Heading back out, swap. At night they flash red or green to match.

EdgeComing in from seaLight
Port sidered can (flat top)red flashes
Starboard sidegreen cone (pointed top)green flashes

2. Black-and-yellow danger buoys ("cardinals"). Two cones on top tell you which way the danger lies; the light tells you it's a cardinal. Pass on the named side:

BuoyConesLight (white)
Northboth upconstant quick flashes
Eastone up, one down3 quick flashes
Southboth down6 quick + 1 long flash
Westboth sideways9 quick flashes

Memory trick: think of a clock face — 3, 6, 9 o'clock for East, South, West.

3. Black-and-red striped buoy — the danger is right underneath it. Flash-flashes twice. Give it a wide berth.

4. Red-and-white striped buoy — safe water all around, often the start of a channel. Pass any side.

5. All-yellow buoy — "special": a zone with its own rules. Check your chart or the app for what it means.

Two marks in a line? That's a transit — keep the two marks lined up and you're on the safe path. If they drift apart, you're sliding off. (Same trick tells you whether your anchor is holding — Module 5.)

The wreck buoy — blue and yellow, flashing alternately — marks a new wreck until it's cleared or permanent marks are set. Keep well clear; a fresh wreck is an uncharted hazard.

Orange-and-black posts and buoys mark ski lanes — go straight through on the right side of the lane. Black-and-white ones mark reserved areas. And never tie your boat to any marker — they're there to keep people alive, not hold your boat.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKHeading home at dusk, you pass a green flashing cone. Which side of the channel are you on? (Green marks the right side coming in from sea — so heading home you're passing it on your left.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA yellow-black buoy with two cones pointing UP, flashing constantly. Where's the danger? (It's the North mark — the danger sits south of it. Pass on its northern side.)

Lesson 3.8 — Flags, signals & spelling over the radio ≈ 8 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTSTILLS - M3-05 flag cards + phonetic list

The divers flag. White over blue, at least 600 mm big, visible from over 200 metres. Boats with divers in the water must fly it — and everyone stays at 5 knots or slower within 200 m. Diving or snorkelling off your boat? Fly it.

The red flag. Red flag on a big ship = tanker or explosives cargo. Red light at night. Stay 200 m away.

Six flags worth knowing:

FlagMeaning
ADiver down — stay away, go slow
BDangerous cargo aboard — keep clear
OMan overboard
VI need help
WI need a doctor
N over CDistress

Signals you might see — or use: red hand flares, orange smoke, constant horn blasts, an orange cloth, arms waving slowly up and down, a burning flame, an EPIRB going off. If you see any of these — help if you safely can, and raise the alarm.

Horn signals — two separate sets. When boats are in sight of each other: one short blast = "I'm turning right"; two short blasts = "I'm turning left"; three short blasts = "I'm going backwards"; five short blasts = "I don't understand what you're doing — danger". In fog or poor visibility: a boat making way — moving through the water — sounds one long blast every two minutes; a boat under way but stopped — not moving through the water, and not anchored — sounds two long blasts, a couple of seconds apart. An anchored boat doesn't sound these horn signals. You'll hear these from other boats — and five quick blasts is the simplest way to tell another skipper their move makes no sense.

Spelling over the radio: the phonetic alphabet stops "M" and "N" sounding the same: Alfa Bravo Charlie Delta Echo Foxtrot Golf Hotel India Juliett Kilo Lima Mike November Oscar Papa Quebec Romeo Sierra Tango Uniform Victor Whiskey X-ray Yankee Zulu. Your boat's radio name — "Zulu Mike Echo" — is spoken this way.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA red flag hanging from a big ship ahead. What is it and what do you do? (Dangerous cargo — tanker or explosives. Stay 200 m away.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKSpell "ZME" for the radio. (Zulu Mike Echo.)

Lesson 3.9 — Alcohol, kids & towing ≈ 5 min

  • »»Alcohol: the skipper stays sober. Alcohol hits twice as hard on the water — sun, motion, spray. Both the patrol and the club will act on it.
  • »»Kids driving: the law is about what the boat can do: if a boat can go faster than 10 knots, nobody under 15 drives it — unless someone over 15 (so at least 16) is directly supervising from within arm's reach of the controls. (It doesn't matter how slowly you're actually going — if the boat can do it, the rule applies.) On SBC boats it's stricter: the person who booked must be 16 or older, passengers don't drive unless the skipper asks, and kids-at-the-wheel practice only happens with the club's say-so at induction.
  • »»Towing a skier or biscuit: the person being towed wears a lifejacket. If you tow above 5 knots, someone 10 or older must be aboard who tells the driver immediately if anything goes wrong. No towing after dark. 5 knots in the slow zones. On our boats: the ski pole is for skiers and wakeboarders only — tubes and biscuits tow from a bridle on both back cleats.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYour 13-year-old takes the wheel while you sit in the bow; the boat is rolling along at a gentle 5 knots. The boat can do 35 knots. What's wrong with this picture? (Under 15 — the rule looks at what the boat CAN do, not how fast you're going today. And the supervising person must be over 15 — so at least 16 — within arm's reach of the controls. Plus it's not how SBC boats operate at all.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKOne more biscuit run at 20:30, sunset at 20:40? (Fine — lifejacket on, watcher aboard, done before dark. Towing stops at sunset, every time.)

Lesson 3.10 — Fishing rules & protected places ≈ 8 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTSTILLS - M3-07 reserve boundary + measure stick + official app

Fishing is half the reason people join SBC — and it has its own rulebook, from two places. Fisheries rules (sizes, daily limits, closures) come from the government's fisheries ministry (MPI). On-the-water rules (speed, ski lanes, obstructions) come from the Auckland Council navigation bylaw — the 5-knot material you already know from Lessons 3.2 and 3.7. Same habit covers both: check before you go, every time.

No licence needed — recreational sea fishing in New Zealand is free for everyone. Free doesn't mean rule-less: every species has minimum sizes and daily bag limits, they differ between areas, and they change. The area matters: all three SBC marinas sit in the Auckland (East) management area — the Hauraki Gulf side. The west coast (Kaipara, Manukau) is a different area with different numbers. Check the official app before every trip — the MPI fishing rules app works offline at the ramp: pick your area (Auckland East), pick the species, see today's limits. Never run last year's numbers.

Measure, don't guess. A plastic measure stick costs a few dollars — carry one. Undersized fish go back promptly, whatever the species. Keep your catch whole onboard: size limits need measurable fish (the club's no-filleting rule in Module 4 does the same job). Take only what you'll actually eat.

Rock lobsters (crayfish) are measured differently from finfish — by weight and body length, and the rules differ by species and by area. Check the app for today's rules, and keep them whole until you're home (the club's no-filleting rule covers all catch). And toheroa is closed by default — it can't be taken unless MPI declares an open season; check the current rules before you even think about it.

Marine reserves — absolute no-take. In our patch: Goat Island (Leigh), Tāwharanui, Long Bay-Okura and Te Matuku (Waiheke) — plus the one you can't miss from Westhaven: Motu Manawa–Pollen Island, 500 hectares of the inner Waitematā Harbour right next door. Inside a reserve boundary: no fishing of any kind, and no taking or harming any sea life — including shells, seaweed and rocks. Don't feed the fish. Swimming, snorkelling and boating through are fine. Ignoring this is prosecuted: in August 2026, three fishers were each fined $600 for fishing inside a marine reserve.

Temporary closures and rāhui are live rules too. As at October 2026: no mussels, pāua or packhorse cray within one nautical mile of Waiheke Island (in place until August 2028); cockles closed at Umupuia Beach; cockles, mussels, oysters and pipi closed at Te Mātā and Waipatukahu on the Coromandel side; and two-year rockpool collection bans around Whangaparāoa, Kawau Bay and Ōmaha. Several of these are rāhui — closures requested by iwi and put in place through the fisheries rules, so these particular closures are enforceable; they also carry real cultural weight. The boundaries and any changes are mapped on MPI's website — treat them like a chart.

Selling or trading catch is illegal — recreationally caught seafood cannot be sold, bought or swapped. Full stop.

See something wrong? Undersized fish kept, lines in a reserve, nets where they shouldn't be: call 0800 4 POACHER (0800 476 224) — anonymous, and it gets acted on.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou're anchored inside the reserve boundary at Goat Island and a passenger hooks a snapper. What now? (Release it — inside a reserve nothing may be taken, even by accident. Move outside the boundary before anyone drops a line.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKHalf the crew wants pipi from the rocks at Army Bay for dinner. Fine? (No — rockpool collection is banned around Whangaparāoa right now, and shellfish rules change often: check the app for that spot, that day, every time.)
