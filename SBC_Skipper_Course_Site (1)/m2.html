Module 2 - Before you go

SBC Skipper · Module 2 of 8 · ≈ 60 minutes · Quiz: 10 questions, 80% to pass

Most bad days on the water were decided at the dock. This module is the seamanship that keeps trips boring — in the best way: lifejackets that fit, a boat checked over, a forecast actually read, a tide understood, and a trip plan someone ashore knows about.

Lesson 2.1 — Lifejackets: the rule that does the saving ≈ 10 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M2-01 lifejacket donning + child fit

The SBC rule is simple: every person wears a properly secured lifejacket whenever the boat is underway. Take it off only to swim off a stopped boat.

Some context worth knowing: since 9 October 2026, New Zealand requires exactly this standard on recreational craft of 6 m or less — it's how the country now expects small boats to be run. On SBC boats it's a condition of membership, written into your terms and the handbook, so the practical rule for you is the same either way: wear it. And the reason is the same in every case: a person in the water wearing a lifejacket floats and is findable; without one, they may sink quickly. Rescue times are measured in minutes.

Heightened risk = wear, no exceptions: rough seas, poor visibility, adverse weather, or any emergency — jackets on, properly secured.

What's on board: six 175 N manually-inflating adult jackets (175 N is the buoyancy rating — the force the jacket provides to keep you afloat) and one child foam jacket (22–40 kg), under the port passenger seat. More than one child aboard? Bring extra — the boat can carry 6 people but it carries one child jacket. Your own jackets are welcome as long as everyone has a correctly fitting one.

Fitting — do it properly, out loud, in front of everyone:

  1. Loosen the straps, put it on, fasten and cinch tight — it should not lift past your ears when a mate pulls up on the shoulders.
  2. Inflatables: check the gas cylinder is seated and the manual inflate pull is accessible. Show everyone the handle and explain what it does — but never fire it on a serviceable jacket: pulling the handle spends the CO2 cylinder and takes that jacket out of service until it's rearmed. If you want to demonstrate a live inflation, use a designated training jacket.
  3. Children: size by weight, not age, and buckle every strap. Foam jackets for kids — no inflatables for small children.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA passenger clips their inflatable but leaves both side straps loose. What do you do? (Re-fit it — an unsecured inflatable rides up and can trap you in the water.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKTwo kids, 30 kg and 25 kg, plus four adults. What's missing? (One more child jacket — bring it.)

Lesson 2.2 — The skipper's briefing ≈ 6 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M2-02 the skipper's briefing

Good skippers run the same brief before every trip — every passenger, every time, before lines come off. Two minutes aboard that replaces a dozen mid-trip explanations. Run yours in this order:

  1. Vessel orientation — where people sit, what things are, where hands should be.
  2. Safety gear locations — lifejackets, throw rope, EPIRB, extinguisher, grab bag. Show them; don't just point.
  3. Lifejacket fitting demo — everyone fitted properly before you cast off (Lesson 2.1).
  4. VHF — where it is, that it's always powered, Ch 16 — and that anyone may use it in an emergency.
  5. The house rules — seated while underway, limbs inboard, no bow riding ever, wait for instructions before boarding or stepping off.
  6. Onboard hazards — the windlass (the powered anchor winch on the bow — fingers, hair and loose items well clear), the propeller when swimming, cleats underfoot, hatches shut and loose gear stowed.
  7. Medical — quietly ask about anything that could matter. Seasickness is the funny one; allergies and heart conditions are not.
  8. Emergencies — what "Overboard! Overboard!" means (point and shout), where the grab bag lives, and that you give instructions — their job is to listen and stay seated.
  9. Clothing — sun and spray protection; it's always colder out there than on the dock.

One more habit from the club's briefing standard: passengers don't touch the helm or controls unless you ask — especially when coming alongside.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA passenger starts climbing forward while you're on the plane. What did your briefing miss? (The seated-underway rule and the bow-riding ban — brief it before you leave, enforce it gently on the water.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKOrder the brief: orientation → safety gear locations → lifejacket demo → onboard hazards → medical → emergencies.

Lesson 2.3 — Pre-departure checks: the 5-minute walk ≈ 8 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M2-03 checklist on camera

A fixed routine catches problems at the dock, where they're cheap. Work top to bottom every time — no skips, no "she was fine last week":

  1. Batteries — house and start ON, voltage over 12 V.
  2. Bilges — clear, pump tested at the helm, discharge visible overboard.
  3. Fuel — full, with your reserve planned (Module 1 maths).
  4. Trim & steering — trim switches work, steering lock to lock.
  5. Engine run — tell-tale flowing, temps and pressures normal, discharge clear.
  6. Radio — powered on (it lives on the battery circuit), volume and squelch set.
  7. Electronics — GPS position sane, sounder on, alarms set.
  8. Safety gear — lifejackets, EPIRB, extinguisher, torch, throw rope, grab bag contents.
  9. Wet locker — buckets, bailer (a scoop for bailing water), sponge, cleaning kit present.
  10. Anchoring system — safety clip and strop (the strap that secures the anchor) in place.
  11. Lights — navigation and anchor light tested.
  12. Inventory — bait board, ski pole, boat hook, spare lines, front hatch secured.
  13. Stowage — loose gear secured where it can't fly or slide.

Found a fault? Report it, and don't operate until it's fixed or the club confirms it's safe. "She'll be right" is how small problems become towing stories.

(Club note: the app check-in wraps around these checks — hull photos, selfie, declared people count, lockbox code. The clicks live in Appendix A; the seamanship lives here.)

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou find the bilge pump runs but nothing comes out of the discharge. Go or no-go? (No-go until reported — a pump that runs dry isn't pumping.)

Lesson 2.4 — Weather: reading the day like a skipper ≈ 12 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M2-04 marine forecast reading

A marine forecast is not a land forecast. Learn its five parts:

  • »»Wind — direction (where it's from) and speed in knots. Expect gusts up to 50% higher than the average, and headlands funnel wind to roughly double it. 25 kn forecast = 35+ kn gusts in the wrong spots.
  • »»Sea vs swell — sea is local wind waves; swell is the long-period leftover from distant weather. Swell steepens in shallow water.
  • »»Wave height words — smooth (<0.5 m), slight (<1 m), moderate (<2 m), rough (<3 m). For a 5.95 m trailerboat, "moderate" is already a working day; "rough" is not a day.
  • »»Visibility — you only get a mention when it's bad: poor (1–3 nm), fog (<1 nm). Fog = no-go on these boats.
  • »»Warnings — strong wind: steady 25+ kn (gusts 33); gale: 33–46; storm: 47+. A strong-wind advisory means stay home or find sheltered water.

Where to look: MetService marine app/website, Coastguard app, Windy or PredictWind for models. On the water: Maritime Radio broadcasts forecasts at 0133, 0533, 0733, 1333, 1733, 2133 — announced on Ch 16, then go to the regional working channel — and Coastguard NowCasting gives continuous conditions (your local channel is in the Coastguard app).

Wind against tide = ugly. When wind blows against an outgoing tide the sea steepens sharply — same wind, double the nastiness. Check both before you commit.

The club's operating bands (they'll be shown for today in the app):

ForecastOperating limit
0–20 knAuckland Inshore Limits
20–30 knEnclosed Water Limits (25 kn cap with easterlies or wind against tide)
30 kn +All bookings cancelled

Management can impose Enclosed Limits any time the forecast wobbles — treat it as a floor, not a challenge.

The go/no-go habit: forecast + sea state + your crew's experience + your daylight window. Any doubt on any one of them = change the plan. Cancellations are free until 6 am — if in doubt, don't go out.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKForecast: 12 kn SW, sea slight, tide turning against the wind at 3 pm. What's the smart window? (Morning — wind-against-tide after 3 pm will steepen the sea.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKForecast says 28 kn with 40 kn gusts. Which limit applies? (Enclosed Water Limits — and honestly, gusts near 40 suggest the couch is the better destination.)

Lesson 2.5 — Tides: the water moves, plan for it ≈ 8 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTSCREEN - M2-06 tide times lookup

Tides change three things you care about: how much water is under you, which way the water flows, and how rough the wind makes it.

  • »»Times & heights — every tide app shows them. Note today's low and high and plan around the low if you're going shallow.
  • »»Springs vs neaps — around full and new moon the range is biggest (springs); quarter moons make it small (neaps). Shallow bays behave very differently between them.
  • »»Streams — water flows into and out of every harbour, river and channel. Going against a strong stream at 5 kn is a treadmill; crossing one drags you sideways.
  • »»Depth — an outgoing tide can drop half a metre-plus under your hull while you fish. Your anchoring cap is 10 m maximum depth (5–6 m ideal) — check the tide before you drop, and if the tide is going out, anchor in slightly deeper water to compensate. Incoming? A little more chain.
  • »»Wind against tide — the sea state lesson's ugly sister; check both forecasts and tide times together.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKHigh tide 10:34, low tide 16:47. You want a shallow sandy bay at 16:00. What's the play? (Depth will be near its lowest — check the tide drop and either go earlier or anchor slightly deeper off the shore.)

Lesson 2.6 — Charts & marks: reading the water's map ≈ 8 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTSTILLS - M2-07 chart marks + plotter route

A chart is a map whose most important feature is what's under the boat:

  • »»Soundings — depths are measured from chart datum (roughly the lowest astronomical tide), not from today's water. Actual depth now = charted depth + today's tide height — check the datum note on the chart and add the tide from your app before trusting under-keel clearance. Read soundings along your route, not just at the destination.
  • »»Contours — lines of equal depth; tight spacing = steep drop-offs, wide spacing = flats that dry out.
  • »»Dotted/declared zones — cable zones (no anchoring/fishing), marine reserves (5 kn), marine farms, mooring areas.
  • »»Lateral marks (IALA-A, used in NZ) — coming in from open sea: red cans to port, green cones to starboard. Going the other way, flip it. The rule of thumb: "red-right-returning" is for America — not here.
  • »»Cardinal marks — pass on the named side: North mark = north of the danger, and so on.
  • »»Lit marks — for dusk edges; but remember your night rule — you shouldn't be out after dark anyway.

On the plotter: set waypoints along your route (not one giant leap), keep the depth alarm on, and glance at soundings where the alarm might matter. Paper backup: Chart 532 and the charting kit live in the grab bag — electronics fail; charts don't.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou're entering a harbour from the sea. Which side do you leave the red cans on? (Port — IALA-A. Green cones to starboard.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKThe chart shows a cable zone across your shortcut. What now? (No anchoring or fishing there — go around; the shortcut isn't worth a cut cable and a fine.)

Lesson 2.7 — Trip plan & trip reports: someone always knows ≈ 8 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M2-05 trip report filed in app

The plan is 5 lines: where you're going, roughly when, who's aboard, how much fuel that takes, and what the weather window is. Do it even for a "quick fish" — especially for a quick fish.

The Trip Report is how that plan reaches people who can act on it. File it with Coastguard before you leave — through the Coastguard app, or by VHF if you hold a Radio Operator Certificate (anyone may use VHF in an emergency, but routine reporting goes via the app for everyone else). Contents: people on board, brief plan, ETA, listening channel (Ch 16).

Closing the loop: close the Trip Report within 30 minutes of getting back. If you don't, an overdue procedure starts. If you're late back against your booking: the club sees it on the plotter and calls you first, and if you can't be reached, Coastguard is asked to raise you and — if that fails — to begin a search with your last known position. That system works because you filed the report.

Two independent ways to communicate, always: the VHF (battery-wired, broadcast to everyone listening) plus your phone (*500 for Coastguard assistance, 111 in an emergency for Police/SAR). Know what you'd do if each one failed.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou're back at 15:20, exhausted. The Trip Report can wait until after the boat's washed... or can it? (No — close it within 30 minutes of return, or the overdue procedure starts.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKVHF fails mid-trip. What are your two fallbacks? (Mobile phone — *500 for Coastguard assistance, 111 for emergencies — and the plotter/app, which keeps the club tracking you.)
