Module 1 - Your boat: the SF545

SBC Skipper · Module 1 of 8 · ≈ 45 minutes · Quiz: 10 questions, 80% to pass

Every SBC boat is the same boat. Twenty-four Haines Hunter SF545s — same engine, same plotter, same bait board in the same place. Learn one and you know twenty-four, whichever marina you're at. This module walks you around your boat: what it is, how it starts, what feeds it, what powers it, where everything lives.

Member responsibilities reminder: you return the boat full of fuel, hosed down and clean. Cleaning gear is in the wet locker.

Lesson 1.1 — Hull & layout ≈ 8 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M1-01 slow walkaround
»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M1-02 cockpit 360°

The SF545 is a 5.95-metre New Zealand-built sportfisher with a 21° deep-vee hull — the shape that cuts through chop instead of slamming over it. It has a walk-around cabin, a full Bimini (the folding shade canopy) for shade, and a self-draining cockpit: any water that comes over the side drains out through the scuppers (the drain holes along the deck edge) on its own. Keep the drain holes clear — a bucket or a bait board shoved over a scupper stops the self-draining part.

How many people? Your Certificate of Survey rating is 6 persons including you, the skipper. That's the legal maximum — and it's a weight-and-stability number, so spread people and gear sensibly rather than piling everyone in the stern.

One design, one fleet: all 24 hulls are identical inside. Each has its own name on the side (Taika, Ari, Kai, Tane…) and its own MNZ number — quote the name or number if you ever call the club about that boat. Two words you'll use all day: port = left, starboard = right (looking forward).

Lesson 1.2 — The engine & drive ≈ 8 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M1-03 engine start + kill cord
»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M1-04 trim & tilt demo

Yamaha 115 HP outboard, petrol. Enough to plane the boat loaded; gentle enough to handle like a trolley at idle.

The kill cord. At the ignition you'll find a lanyard with a clip — the kill cord. Clip it to you, every trip. If you move away from the helm or fall, it pulls out and the engine stops. It has saved more lives than any other gadget on small boats.

Starting. Key to ON first — the engine display wakes up showing trim position, fuel level and battery voltage (the same data mirrors on the plotter). Check those, then start. After starting, look for the tell-tale — the little stream of cooling water out the side. No tell-tale = no cooling = shut down and investigate.

Trim & tilt. Two switches: one on the throttle, one on the engine itself as a backup. Trim right down for leaving the berth — your marina parking spot — and getting up onto the plane (skimming on top of the water) with maximum steerage and easy take-off, then trim up as you run for a drier, faster, more comfortable ride. Tilt fully up for berthing, beaching and shallow water. Remove the bait board before tilting — the engine hits it and damages the cowling (the engine's cover).

Steering is checked lock to lock at every pre-departure check. If steering feels stiff or catches, report it — don't head out with a steering problem.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou want to tilt the engine fully up. What must you remove first? (The bait board.)

Lesson 1.3 — Fuel system ≈ 7 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M1-05 fuel gauge + fuel dock fill

130-litre tank, 91/95 petrol. The gauge is divided into blocks — each block ≈ 13 litres. [VERIFY] Fuel-dock electrics — pending confirmation by the club's fuel-dock procedure/operator: the plotter (the chart screen at the helm) shows its dashboard bars for what's going in with the key ON — that demo is filmed at the berth, before or after fuelling. During the fill itself, ignition and electrics stay OFF until confirmed.

The fill sequence. However your marina runs it, the non-negotiables: engine OFF and electrics OFF during the fill, no naked flames (including cigarettes), the extinguisher out and to hand, only the fuelling crew aboard, don't overfill the tank, absorbent cloths ready for spills, windows and hatches closed so fumes can't enter, and portable tanks filled ashore — never aboard. [The electrics rule is pending confirmation against the club's fuel-dock SOP.]

Planning fuel. Real fleet telemetry (45 Westhaven trips, Jun–Oct 2026) gives you two magic numbers:

  • »»≈ 1.06 litres per nautical mile
  • »»21.6 kn average open-water speed, plus ~14 min each way for marina and harbour limits

So: time ≈ 14 min + (distance ÷ 21.6 kn) and fuel ≈ 1.06 L × distance. Worked examples from the club table: Kawau (29.5 nm) = 1 h 35 m and 31 L one way; Rakino (14.5 nm) = 55 min and 15 L; Motuihe (10.5 nm) = 45 min and 11 L.

The golden rule: leave with a full tank and always plan a reserve — a rough guide is a third out, a third back, a third spare. Boats that run out of fuel aren't having emergencies; they're having arithmetic problems. You check fuel is full at pre-departure and refuel before you return. (Club note: upload the receipt photo in the app.)

The fuel system: priming bulb at the fuel filter (squeeze until firm if the engine runs rough after idling), and an emergency fuel shut-off — know where it is and how to close it (shown at induction; your insurance against a fuel leak or engine fire).

Lesson 1.4 — Electrics ≈ 6 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M1-06 battery switch panel

Two batteries, two jobs: the start battery spins the engine; the house battery runs the plotter, sounder, lights and stereo. At pre-departure, both switches ON and voltage over 12 V on the instruments.

The parallel switch sits beside them. If the start battery is flat, it links house to start for an emergency start. Turn it back OFF once the engine is running — the alternator (the engine's generator, ≈ 13 V) then recharges the start battery on its own.

Bilge pump: switch at the helm, discharge outlet on the starboard side. A float switch runs it automatically if water collects. Listen for it working and watch the discharge at your checks — and never run it dry (that kills pumps).

Lights: navigation lights for underway, anchor light when anchored in low light. Test both at pre-departure.

What flattens batteries: the stereo, plotter left on, 12 V sockets. The shutdown routine turns everything off — do it every time. A flat battery strands the next trip, possibly yours.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKEngine won't crank, start battery reads 11.4 V. What's the fix? (Parallel switch ON, start, then parallel OFF once running.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKWhich battery runs the stereo? (House.)

Lesson 1.5 — Electronics ≈ 6 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M1-07 plotter boot + waypoint set

The plotter wakes with the first click of the key — engine not needed. First habit: confirm the GPS position looks right against where you actually are. Electronics are aids, not truth; your sanity check catches stale or wrong positions before you rely on them.

The core skills (shown at induction, practised here):

  • »»Heads-up vs north-up display — pick one and stay consistent.
  • »»MOB function: tap the screen, save the position. That saved point is how you steer straight back to a person in the water.
  • »»Depth sounder: helm switch turns on depth + fishfinder. Set the depth alarm — it's your warning before the hull meets the bottom.
  • »»Waypoints and go-to: tap a destination, save it, navigate to it.

The day's operating limit is drawn on the plotter and shown on the Charts page of the SBC app — it's the line you stay inside (limits and why they change are Module 2). The plotter also reports to the club, so if you're ever overdue or in trouble, help already knows where to look.

One rule from the Conditions of Use: plotting while moving at speed is how people hit things. Slow down or stop to use the plotter — like you would with a phone in the car.

Lesson 1.7 — Lines, fenders & cleats ≈ 6 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M1-10 cleats, lines, fenders detail

Lines. One knot does the dock work: the bowline. Tie it in the end of the line and slip the loop over a horn of the dock cleat — holds hard, releases instantly, one hand. (The seven knots — cleat hitch included — are taught in Module 5.) Coiling: loop the line in neat clockwise circles so it runs freely without kinks and stows ready to throw.

Fenders. They only work at the right height: hang them so the fattest part meets the dock at dock level, not at gunwale level (the top edge of the boat's side), and put one at the point of contact fore (front) and aft (back) of where the hull will touch. Tie them to a cleat or rail — never to a lifeline that can't take the load.

Before you get on the plane: fenders in and stowed, lines coiled and stowed, bait board stowed, front hatch secured, loose gear stowed in the cabin. A fender that flies off at 20 knots becomes someone else's emergency.

When you berth: bow line, stern line and two springs (the diagonal lines that stop the boat sliding forward or back) — that's the shutdown standard, because four points hold the boat without loading any one line.
