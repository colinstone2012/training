Module 7 - Emergencies

SBC Skipper · Module 7 of 8 · ≈ 60 minutes · Quiz: 12 questions served from a 36-question bank, 80% to pass

Most emergencies on small boats are short: seconds to decide, minutes to resolve, and the first two actions usually decide the outcome. This module is the sequence for each one — the same procedures the SBC Handbook carries, taught until the first move is reflex.

Lesson 7.1 — The skipper mindset ≈ 5 min

Three things, before any specific emergency:

  • »»You are responsible for everyone aboard. On SBC boats the named member skipper is aboard and in charge — that responsibility doesn't pause when things go wrong.
  • »»The sequence is always: STOP – SECURE – COMMUNICATE. Stop the boat or the cause; secure the people (lifejackets, positions); then communicate. Panic skips straight to communicating — don't.
  • »»Prevention is most of it: complete pre-departure checks every time, stow everything, brief more than one person on the plotter and VHF (never assume you'll be the one able to operate them), and remind the crew of onboard hazards — windlass, cleats, lines, the propeller — as you go.

The club's reporting chain runs through the same app you check in with: incidents, damage, first-aid items used — all reported so the next crew and the record are safe.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKWhat are the three beats of the emergency sequence? (Stop – Secure – Communicate.)

Lesson 7.2 — Man overboard ≈ 8 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M7-02 staged MOB drill

The crew drill (everyone aboard knows it): see someone fall in — point at them and shout "Overboard! Overboard!" and keep pointing. A pointing finger is the fastest direction-finder on a small boat.

The skipper drill, in order:

  1. Throw flotation immediately — fenders, then the throw rope from the port cubby. At night, throw a light or reflective item too.
  2. Mark the position: tap the plotter and save the MOB point — that's what you steer back to if you lose sight.
  3. Mayday or Pan-Pan on Ch 16 if there's any doubt about the person's location or condition. Sea state and time both argue for calling early.
  4. Slow the boat, turn, and return — crew prepares the medical kit and dry clothing.
  5. A passenger swims to assist only on the skipper's instruction, wearing a lifejacket.
  6. If not found: run a search pattern based on wind and tide drift — downwind and downstream of the mark.
  7. Engine OFF alongside — the swim ladder sits next to the propeller. Recovery over the stern or ladder, then treat for cold and shock.

Why minutes matter — cold water: falling into NZ water (even in summer, 16-18°C) starts a rough survival clock — the famous 1-10-1 rule is a guide, not a stopwatch, because cold shock and hypothermia vary a lot with the person, clothing and sea state: roughly 1 minute to get your breathing under control (cold shock — don't gasp, don't thrash); about 10 minutes of meaningful movement before hands and arms stop working; and maybe an hour before hypothermia can render you unconscious. Don't count on those numbers — count on flotation. Stay with the boat (it floats higher than you), get as much of your body out of the water as you can, and the HELP position (knees to chest, arms folded) slows heat loss if you can't. That's the whole case for the lifejacket rule: it buys you the minutes and keeps you findable while they pass.

The lifejacket connection: a person wearing one floats and is findable. That is why the club rule exists.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKBuild the first 60 seconds: shout → throw → plotter → Mayday/Pan-Pan if doubt → return → engine off at pickup.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKWhy engine off for the pickup? (The swim ladder is beside the propeller — a running engine at the pickup point is a second casualty.)

Lesson 7.3 — Engine failure ≈ 7 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M7-03 engine-fail response

Modern outboards need voltage, fuel and cooling. When power dies:

  1. Anchor immediately if you're drifting toward danger — rocks, channel, moored boats. An anchored boat is a safe boat.
  2. Check the engine temperature dial and inspect fuel lines for breaks or leaks.
  3. Confirm battery power — if the start battery is low, switch on the parallel switch (next to the battery switches) to link the house battery for the start attempt.
  4. Prime the fuel with the bulb next to the fuel filter; check there's fuel in the filter.
  5. Fouled propeller? Engine off, key out of the ignition before anyone clears the prop.
  6. Restart. If she won't run: assess the danger level, then call Coastguard on *500 and arrange a tow — and inform SBC shore base (after hours: 09 475 6444). Mayday on Ch 16 only if you're drifting into grave and imminent danger.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKEngine dies, you're drifting toward rocks 100 m off. First action? (Anchor immediately — stopping the drift comes before diagnosis.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKStart battery reads 11.2 V. How do you attempt the restart? (Parallel switch ON to link the house battery, attempt start, parallel OFF once the alternator is charging.)

Lesson 7.4 — Fire & fuel ≈ 6 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M6-06 refresher

Fires start at the switchboard or the engine — fuel or electrical. The kit: dry powder extinguisher, starboard side wall at the helm.

Engine fire:

  1. Shout "FIRE! FIRE! FIRE!" — everyone away from the smoke, upwind.
  2. Stop the outboard; shut the fuel supply (remote shut-off); isolate the batteries.
  3. Discharge the dry powder into the cowling air intake — never remove the cowling (that feeds the fire air).
  4. Discharge it fully. Partial sprays don't put out fuel fires.
  5. Not out? Mayday on Ch 16, *500, SBC shore base — and prepare the worst case while you still have choices.

Fuel discipline prevents most of this: nozzles up when moving the pump, rags ready, watch the breather, absorbent mats down. Smell fuel? Engine off, find it, fix it, ventilate before starting.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKWhere does the powder go, and what must you never do? (Into the cowling air intake; never remove the cowling — it feeds the fire air.)

Lesson 7.5 — Swamping & flooding ≈ 6 min

Water inside the boat comes from collision, a failed fitting, or waves over the bow. It threatens stability first — a boat with water in one side heels, and heeling brings more water in.

Three different emergencies: swamping — water coming in over the sides or bow, boat still upright; capsize — the boat overturns (a big beam wave, or the bow burying and the stern flipping over — "turn turtle") but trapped air and buoyancy keep it afloat; sinking — the worst case, nothing left to hold onto. If capsized and close to shore isn't an option: climb onto the overturned hull — far easier to spot than a swimmer, and out of the water delays hypothermia.

And the first move: stop the boat. Water pressure against a moving hull drives faster ingress — every flooding drill starts by coming off the plane.

  1. Bilge pumps on (helm switch; check the discharge is actually running).
  2. Send the message early — Ch 16 to Coastguard and nearby vessels, and SBC shore base. Situations deteriorate fast; you may not get another chance.
  3. Head for shallower water or the shore — beaching a flooding boat is a legitimate last resort (Module 5 technique, engine off at the sand).
  4. Stem the flow: block the hole (grab bag, cushions, whatever holds), shut any valves.
  5. Freeing ports clear — water on the deck must be able to drain out.
  6. Balance the weight against the heel.
  7. Prepare to abandon ship — but stay aboard as long as she floats. The boat is your biggest life-raft.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKWater over the sole, pumps keeping up. Do you still call? (Yes — send the message early. If the pumps lose, you want help already moving.)

Lesson 7.6 — Grounding ≈ 5 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M7-04 grounding response

You felt the bottom. Don't reverse blindly:

  1. Check for damage and injury; lifejackets on.
  2. Refloat only if there is no water ingress: trim the outboard up slightly, gentle astern, building gradually until she comes clear.
  3. Taking water? Bilge pumps on and re-evaluate — she only refloats if the pumps keep up or there's no ingress.
  4. Can't refloat safely: Coastguard on VHF or *500, and SBC shore base — wait for assistance. With help, if she still can't come free, the skipper may decide to abandon.
  5. After any grounding: props and skeg checked (photos in-app), report it.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou're hard aground on sand at low tide, hull intact. Refloat now or wait? (Wait for the tide if there's no urgency — she'll float off; forcing it off at the wrong moment risks the prop and the weekend.)

Lesson 7.7 — Vessel collision ≈ 5 min

  1. Raise the alarm and take command — brief the passengers.
  2. Injuries first — a casualty outranks the boat.
  3. Assess the damage: above or below the waterline?
  4. If vessels are impaled, do not pull apart — that's what turns a dent into a sinking.
  5. Mayday/Pan-Pan as required on Ch 16; advise Coastguard and SBC shore base: collision, position, POB, assistance required.
  6. Lifejackets fitted tight; medical kit out; bilge pumps ready.
  7. If the hull is breached and pumps can't keep up — the abandon-ship decision (Lesson 7.9).

And afterwards: report it — Harbourmaster and Maritime NZ where required, SBC in-app, photos.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKYou've clipped a moored boat; both hulls seem stuck together at the rub rail. Pull apart? (No — impaled structures seal the wound. Assess, stabilise, call, and let professionals separate you.)

Lesson 7.8 — Medical emergency ≈ 5 min

  1. Notify the skipper immediately — complete information: what happened, symptoms, existing conditions (family members often know).
  2. Request help: VHF Ch 16 to Coastguard (they route medical advice and can plan a meeting point with an ambulance), or 111 in phone coverage. Give clear location — the plotter position — and the nature of the injury.
  3. Maintain contact until help arrives; update if the condition changes.
  4. Inform SBC shore base (in-app or after-hours line).
  5. Trained passengers treat within their training; monitor continuously.
  6. Collect details for the incident report and report any first-aid items used so the kit is restocked.

Cold water immersion context (Module 2): unconsciousness can come fast — that's why the MOB drill exists and why lifejackets are worn.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKSuspected broken arm, phone coverage, 2 nm from Half Moon Bay. Call whom first? (111 for ambulance coordination with a clear position and meeting point — and keep Ch 16 in the background; inform SBC.)

Lesson 7.9 — Distress calling & abandoning ship ≈ 7 min

»» CLUB MEDIA SLOTVIDEO - M6-02 refresher

Mayday — grave and imminent danger. Full script on Ch 16: "Mayday, Mayday, Mayday — this is [vessel name, callsign ×3] — Mayday [vessel name and callsign] — position — nature of emergency — assistance required — people on board — conditions — Over." Then listen; repeat and activate the EPIRB if there's no response. DSC (if the set has it) sends the same alert digitally. On a phone: 111, Police — don't hang up unless SAR tells you to.

Abandoning ship — the skipper's order only, and only when staying aboard is a bigger risk than the water:

  1. Lifejackets on everyone, properly secured.
  2. Mayday with position on Ch 16.
  3. Call SBC shore base by phone — shore assistance starts from the club too.
  4. Take the grab bag, emergency torch, and EPIRB.
  5. Activate the EPIRB.
  6. Shut all fuel valves; isolate the electrics.
  7. Off the boat in an orderly way — huddle together for warmth and visibility.
  8. Mark the wreck: ~35 m of rope with a fender on the end, tied to the boat — for recovery and as a navigation warning.

The golden rule if a life raft is deployed: never step down into a raft — step up. And stay aboard as long as she floats: the boat is your biggest life-raft.

»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKOrdering abandon ship is the skipper's call — but which single instruction comes FIRST in the sequence? (Lifejackets on and secured — before the radio, before the EPIRB.)
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKWhy tie the rope and fender to the wreck? (It marks the obstruction for other boats and marks the wreck for recovery.)

Lesson 7.10 — Towing, being towed & reporting ≈ 3 min

  • »»Accept tows from Coastguard or the club's approved channels — private tows have caused more damage than they've fixed: wrong gear, wrong point, no insurance cover. Non-urgent: *500 / 0800 BOATIE. Urgent: Ch 16.
  • »»Being towed: lifejackets stay on, crew clear of the towline (a parted line is a blade), steering used to follow the tow.
  • »»Report everything: accidents involving damage or injury go to the Harbourmaster and Maritime NZ where required — and always to SBC in-app. Photos, position, what happened.
»» KNOWLEDGE CHECKA passing boat offers to tow you home before Coastguard arrives. Wise? (Politely decline unless the club/Coastguard arranges it — gear, technique and insurance all matter.)
